<p>Priming Priming refers to the faciliatory processing effects of repeating a stimulus or a task. This ubiquitous phenomenon is of interest to memory researchers because its encoding and retrieval dynamics are both similar to and different from many other forms of learning examined in this volume. This chapter explores one specific domain of priming that has been extensively studied in the memory literature, namely that which occurs when one identifies or classifies a stimulus repeatedly&mdash;referred to as long-term repetition priming. This chapter reviews the mechanisms proposed for the faciliatory effects seen in priming. As the chapter explores each proposed mechanism, it will become clear to the reader that no explanation captures all aspects of priming completely, and as such, it is likely that multiple mechanisms may be working in parallel, with their relative impact on behavior highlighted by the goals of the observer and the context of retrieval. This perspective is captured in the proposed adaptive learning system model of long-term repetition priming.</p>

<p>Introduction When a stimulus or task is repeated, there is often a change in the behavior of an organism. If this change is faciliatory, then the phenomenon is typically referred to as priming or repetition priming . Faciliatory changes with repetition are seen broadly across just about every biological system, such as the immune system, reproductive system, proprioceptive system, muscular system, and neurocognitive system. Although all of these effects could be characterized in the language of learning or plasticity, for the purposes of this chapter, we focus mainly on long-term repetition priming , in which facilitation of subsequent cognitive or perceptual judgments is measurable with even a single prior stimulus exposure and can span delays of minutes to years. The bulk of the scientific literature related to priming focuses on the faciliatory effects that repetition can induce, but there are numerous examples of how stimulus repetition can interfere with subsequent processing of the same or related stimuli. This pattern fits with general principles of cognition and perception, in which plasticity necessarily induces both benefits and costs, depending on future contexts. As such, for the purposes of this chapter, we focus on repetition priming associated with an alteration in subsequent stimulus identification or classification, following initial exposures, that can occur even in the absence of the attempt to explicitly retrieve information about those prior exposures. From this perspective, identifying the contexts in which facilitation and impairment occur is critical for understanding the nature of the underlying cognitive process changes that result from repetition.</p>

<p>Parallel to the behavioral facilitation seen in repetition priming is a neural phenomenon known as repetition suppression . Repetition suppression refers to a decrease in neural activity following stimulus repetition and has been observed in neural firing rates and changes in local field potentials. In addition, there have been corresponding observations across more indirect measures of neural activity, such as electroencephalographic and hemodynamic. Despite the reliable co-occurrence between the behavioral changes seen with priming and repetition suppression, causal relationships have been elusive and difficult to establish. However, given the prevalent use of neuroimaging methods to examine long-term repetition priming, it is important to consider repetition suppression in the context of this chapter, but only to the extent that it informs current conceptualizations of process models of behavioral priming.</p>

<p>To summarize, the main purpose of this chapter is to review a portion of the literature on long-term repetition priming through the lens of information processing and memory. We focus on the successes and limitations of approaches that differ in their assumptions about the adaptive function of repetition priming and also on the relevant dependent measures of priming phenomena that must be explained by a successful theory. As we will show, differences in these core assumptions heavily shape the explanations that are offered, resulting in accounts that can lack specificity with limited predictive capacity. For example, as we note below, some characterizations of repetition priming fail to anticipate future contexts in which behavioral impairment instead of facilitation should occur.</p>

<p>Priming and Representational Theory One of the earliest phenomena linked to repetition priming in the literature was in the context of visual adaptation experiments, in which stimulus repetition was used to better understand putative, neural stimulus representations. The repetition lags in visual adaptation experiments are relatively short&mdash;on the order of milliseconds to tens of seconds. For instance, Blakemore and Campbell discovered the visual adaptation phenomenon during a study of occipital, visual evoked potentials , whereby the repetition of sinusoidal gradients with matched spatial frequency resulted in the maximal reduction of the measured EP response. This finding was subsequently investigated psychophysically, and it was demonstrated that the contrast threshold for detecting the adapted gradient was markedly increased relative to gratings that had not been repeated. This stimulus adaptation approach became widely used in a broad range of studies in order to better understand representational properties throughout the visual processing stream&mdash;such as object representations, motion, and the processing of spatial locations.</p>

<p>Visual adaptation experiments resulted in the formulation of one of the earliest suggested neural explanations of repetition priming&mdash;the fatigue model. Broadly speaking, the fatigue model states that once a set of processers have been &ldquo;activated,&rdquo; in the short term they become desensitized to repeated processing of the same input. The important feature of this model is the formulation that varying the similarity of the test and adapting stimuli along a dimension of interest can thereby reveal important information about the underlying representation coded by the processors. Unfortunately, it is important to note that the fatigue model does not represent a theory of behavioral repetition priming but, rather, stands primarily as a redescription of neurophysiological repetition suppression phenomena. This is important with respect to repetition priming because the construct of neural fatigue offers no clear predictions about behavioral performance&mdash;facilitation, inhibition, and generalization&mdash;and as such its utility for understanding the information processing mechanisms underlying repetition priming is limited.</p>

<p>To better understand the limitations of accounts of neural activation, such as the fatigue model, for understanding repetition priming, it is important to consider exactly what it is that such models purport to explain&mdash;what referred to as the computational theory level. In the case of repetition suppression phenomena linked to visual adaptation experiments, the goal has often been to demonstrate the existence of selective perceptual representations that increase in complexity at different proposed hierarchies of perceptual processing. Representational theories of perception postulate that our experience of the world consists of mapping perceptual inputs onto internal feature vocabularies that can then make whole objects. From this perspective, the selective repetition suppression effects and behavioral declines in detection thresholds that occur during stimulus repetition are not the phenomenon to be explained but, rather, a byproduct of the proposed representational structure that is of primary interest. Experiments which demonstrate that neurons or neural regions show repetition suppression effects to specific stimulus classes only would provide evidence of the uniqueness of that class. For example, the question of whether faces are a uniquely represented class of stimuli is an illustration of this approach. By contrast, showing that these apparently specific neural regions are in fact responsive to other stimulus classes would provide the needed falsification for claims of perceptual specialization. In either case, the focus does not directly address why decision or identification behavior systematically changes with stimulus repetition. Nonetheless, because we will ultimately present a model for investigating repetition priming that incorporates both representational changes and the formation of associations between representations and actions, we briefly review research exploring representational questions in which explaining the behavior observed with repetition plays a key role.</p>

<p>The domain of language is one that readily adopted the use of repetition priming to understand basic underlying neurocognitive structure. In one of the earliest studies, Horowitz and White set about to use a word fragment completion task to better understand the internal &ldquo;organization&rdquo; of single words. Participants studied lists of words&mdash;either six or nine letters long. They were then tested on the latency and accuracy of completing word fragments with letter fragments appearing in different locations. The idea was to test whether different word positions differentially affected their ability to elicit the whole word. The results demonstrated that fragments varied in their utility, with initial and end positions being more useful than the middle of the word (i.e., beginning > ending > middle). The authors used these results to draw conclusions not only about the underlying structure of individual word representations but also about the relationships between words&mdash;namely that words that started similarly would be grouped closer together than words that ended similarly in the mental lexicon. Subsequent priming experiments using lexical decision tasks ultimately supported the latter hypothesis. Following these initial experiments, a broad range of word priming tasks were implemented in cognitive science to understand visual word structure and their underlying lexical and semantic relationships. In fact, findings from the lexical and semantic priming literature led to the influential &ldquo;spreading activation&rdquo; model of priming. With the introduction of human neuroimaging techniques, considerable research emerged that attempted to tie priming effects in language to specific underlying neural systems.</p>

<p>The use of repetition priming to examine underlying representational structure is not restricted to single representational systems. A number of studies used priming to examine the existence and separation of multiple perceptual systems. One important finding of the priming literature was that word priming effects on lexical decision and identification tasks were significantly reduced by a change in study&ndash;test modality. That is, when words were studied auditorily and then tested visually, priming was minimal. The implication of these findings was that perceptual representations of words might be segregated into separate auditory and visual subsystems. Apart from modality, evidence emerged supporting separate organizational systems for abstract versus specific categories of words. Marsolek performed a series of experiments demonstrating that independent subsystems underlie abstract-category and specific-exemplar priming of word forms. This approach to applying priming led to the proliferation of postulated representational &ldquo;subsystems&rdquo; with each new encountered priming effect. Although the use of priming in order to understand the underlying organization of representations is ubiquitous in the literature, less attention was paid to what the underlying mechanism of priming may reveal about how representations are formed and maintained.</p>

<p>Theories that started to emerge in the 1980s postulated that the only way a multitude of perceptual representations could effectively be stored and retrieved in the human brain would be if those representations were distributed, overlapping, and adapted via dynamic learning mechanisms. This particular framework was further extended from concrete perceptions to more abstract-level representations such as semantic knowledge. Subsequently, computational models along with evidence from neuroimaging emerged to support the distributed nature of representations. What is important for the current discussion are the implications of this theoretical perspective for repetition priming. Marsolek articulated that a fundamental feature of a distributed and overlapping representational system is that learning occurs constantly. Exposure to objects or information that have already been represented results in a strengthening of those representations&mdash;referred to as maintenance relearning. By this account, &ldquo;priming is the behaviorally measurable beneficial effect of strengthening some representations on the continued storage of those representations&rdquo;. Although the historical use of priming has often been as a tool to understand the static representational nature of information, this framework firmly connects priming to the actual underlying learning and maintenance of those very same representations. Priming would no longer be considered as an isolated experimental tool but, rather, as reflecting a feature of representational systems that tell us something about how they form, operate, and change. Finally, there is at least one novel prediction of this model&mdash;namely that priming must also come with a cost because the strengthening of some representations would be accompanied by the weakening of other representations with which they are overlapping. Marsolek termed this cost &ldquo;anti-priming&rdquo; and provided computational and neuroimaging support for it ( Figure 10.1 ).</p>

<p>One important aspect of the theoretical perspective that priming reflects changes in representational strength is that it squarely positions priming as a learning process. Moreover, the model is focused on a single level of representation and type of possible plasticity within that representation. This particular learning mechanism may in fact underlie a number of forms of priming, but broader theories of priming should consider the possibility that learning may occur through different mechanisms with different timescales. For instance, it is clear that episodic memory systems support the binding of novel associations between representations, and the role of episodic memory in identification or classification priming must be considered. Finally, perhaps a theory of priming should go beyond whether it reflects one learning system or another and consider the potential for serial, parallel, or interactive functioning of the putative systems.</p>

<p>Memory Systems and Priming Multiple-System Perspective In contrast to using repetition priming as a tool to understand underlying representation or local-level learning processes, the degree to which repetition priming was independent of extensively studied explicit memory processes became a focus of interest. The impetus of this approach was in the late 1960s and early 1970s with the work of Warrington and Weiskrantz, who, in a series of studies, demonstrated that six otherwise densely amnesic patients 1 were nonetheless able to learn and retain a list of fragmented words and pictures when they were repeatedly asked to identify them. In their 1968 paper, Warrington and Weiskrantz observed that these patients did not even remember seeing the study list items and reported simply guessing during the subsequent fragment test. Although earlier work by Milner et al. had shown that amnesic patients could acquire motor skills, what made the word fragment priming task so striking is that the patients were able to show learning with a single trial in a purely cognitive task. This finding had profound implications for theories of learning and memory, and researchers began using priming with the goal of better understanding the nature and number of memory systems.</p>

<p>The finding of preserved repetition priming in amnesia spurred an extensive volume of work beginning in the 1980s which revealed that despite profound anterograde amnesia for events experienced by amnesic patients, many forms of repetition priming remained intact. In addition, researchers examined a host of other individuals with various forms of memory impairment, such as memory declines in normal and pathological aging, patients suffering from chronic alcohol-induced Korsakoff&rsquo;s syndrome, and those suffering from Parkinson&rsquo;s disease. As a result, priming began to be examined extensively through the lens of developing processing theories or systems theories of memory that rested heavily on the logic of experimental dissociations. Systems theories of memory focused on the phenomenological experience of remembering, postulating that explicit memory required some form of conscious remembering of the original episode. By contrast, memory was referred to as &ldquo;implicit&rdquo; when there remained a clear influence on behavior in the absence of consciously remembering the original episode&mdash;such as what occurs in priming. This resulted in theoretical formulations that made a clear distinction between &ldquo;explicit&rdquo; and &ldquo;implicit&rdquo; forms of memory. The dichotomy gave rise to other theoretical formulations that have been influential in the memory literature, such as declarative/nondeclarative and relational/nonrelational. Although these dichotomies do not always align exactly, they capture the basic separation of explicit event memory versus the memory associated with priming.</p>

<p>Despite the focus on interpreting priming as a manifestation of an &ldquo;implicit&rdquo; form of memory, it was never clear that repetition priming in healthy individuals occurred solely in the absence of the ability to explicitly recall or recognize the elements of prior experiences. For instance, in the early work of Graf et al. , it was demonstrated that when participants were given explicit instructions to complete word stems with previously studied words, amnesic patients were impaired relative to healthy controls. This contrasted with the finding of comparable completion patterns between patients and controls when they were given the instructions to simply complete the word stems with the &ldquo;first word that comes to mind.&rdquo; The interpretation of this finding was that the former task instructions explicitly oriented the healthy controls to utilize their episodic memory for previously studies words in the service of the word stem completion task. A major shortcoming of this approach to testing multiple systems was the assumption that task formulation alone could isolate individual systems in a pure manner. Although healthy controls likely could use cues strategically to orient their episodic memory, it is unclear whether other memory processes were being fully &ldquo;turned off&rdquo; or continued to operate in parallel such that changes in task demands could orient a person&rsquo;s attention to a different aspect of the episodes that could then later be retrieved.</p>

<p>Single-System/-Process Perspective In contrast to the multiple memory systems approach to explaining priming, others have argued that many task and neuropsychological dissociations can be better understood as the result of a single memory process that is assessed with varying degrees of efficacy, depending on the sensitivity of the task. If correct, then positing different neural memory systems to explain these dissociations may be unnecessary. Although extensive review of these single-process accounts is beyond the scope of this chapter, we examine two processes crafted to explain dissociations of the ability to perceptually identify words (i.e.., perceptual identification) versus the ability to explicitly recognize these words after they were previously encountered (i.e., explicit recognition).</p>

<p>Perceptual identification and explicit recognition tasks have yielded experimental dissociations across encoding manipulations and also neuropsychological dissociations across amnesic versus control populations. For example, Jacoby and Dallas demonstrated that deeper levels of processing (LOP) of words increased later explicit recognition, but in a second group of subjects, they did not affect the ability to later identify these words when briefly flashed. Although LOP did not improve perceptual identification, these subjects nonetheless identified studied words at a higher rate than novel words. As noted in this early, influential paper, this pattern is consistent with an item-based fluency mechanism supporting both perceptual identification and recognition, versus a context retrieval (i.e., recollection) mechanism supporting only explicit recognition. In the case of fluency, the prior exposure during study is assumed to ease perceptual identification, and this ease of identification during recognition testing (relative to new materials) also biases subjects toward positive recognition conclusions in analogous fashion to availability heuristics in nonmemory judgments. In contrast, the increasingly distinctive experiences produced by the deeper LOP tasks at encoding increase the likelihood of contextual recollection during recognition attempts but do not facilitate basic identification. Turning to a neuropsychological demonstration, Hamann and Squire demonstrated that the densely amnesic patient E.P. produced a numerically higher priming score on a perceptual identification task relative to controls and yet scored at chance for explicit recognition of these materials. These and similar findings suggest that gains in recognition versus perceptual identification rely on different mechanisms.</p>

<p>Critically, however, this conclusion heavily rests on the assumption that perceptual identification performance is truly equivalent across LOP tasks and also equivalent across control versus amnesic groups. Critiquing this assumption, Ostergaard and Jernigan noted that despite the frequent finding of no reliable difference in priming scores across amnesics and controls, they were able to identify at least eight studies documenting impaired performance for amnesics on word-based priming tasks at the time. If amnesics and controls were truly equivalent in priming ability, then these would merely reflect sampling variability across the experiments. However, Ostergaard and Jernigan noted no instances in which amnesics demonstrated reliably superior performance, and thus the pattern instead suggests that low power may explain many of the failures to find reduced priming in amnesics.</p>

<p>The potential sources of low power in neuropsychological studies are numerous and include small sample sizes and the potentially increased variability within patient performance. One particularly pernicious problem, however, might be best described as a missing variable problem in which baseline differences in performance are masked or overlooked by the frequent use of difference scores (novel minus studied) to define priming levels. For instance, Ostergaard found similar levels of perceptual identification priming across amnesics and controls across several conditions when priming was defined as the difference between the identification of novel versus studied materials. However, he also noted that the baseline performance of the amnesic subjects was reliably slower than that of the controls. This would not be problematic if there was no relationship between baseline speed and measured priming; however, within both groups, the priming scores were strongly negatively associated with baseline performance such that the slower individuals had larger priming scores. Although statistical regression to the mean might in part explain the negative relationships within each group, it cannot explain why the amnesic subjects were reliably slower during baseline/novel trials on the task compared to controls. Moreover, when baseline performance was statistically controlled via multiple regression analysis, or the samples were reduced to only those amnesic versus control participants who were closely matched on baseline performance, amnesics subjects were shown to be reliably impaired in priming. In other words, they did not demonstrate the gains in perceptual identification that would be expected to accrue during study for controls who were similarly slow in baseline identification&mdash;a finding consistent with the interpretation that the amnesics were impaired on both implicit and explicit memory tasks. Unfortunately, without a more thorough understanding of why, beyond statistical regression to the mean, baseline performance is linked to priming gains in both controls and amnesics, and a testable explanation of why amnesic participants tend to be unusually slow in baseline performance, this issue will continue to hamper the comparisons of patient and control populations.</p>

<p>Turning to task dissociations within healthy controls, one approach exploring a single-process stance has been to craft statistical decision models capable of mimicking observed dissociations, even though the models only assume a single source of memory evidence. A recent influential model in this regard begins with the assumption that a single memory strength variable underlies both the accuracy of recognition judgments and the speed of perceptual identification. In the case of explicit recognition, this variable ( f &mdash;familiarity) is assumed to have an intrinsic true variability across items ( &#963; f ) that is combined with noise generated by the testing procedure itself ( &#963; r ), yielding a summed variable ( J r ) that is evaluated against decision criterion as illustrated in Figure 10.2 (i.e., J r = f + &#963; r ). When the total evidence exceeds the criterion, the subject claims the item is recognized (yielding hits and false alarms), and when it falls below the criterion, the subject claims it is new to the experiment (yielding correct rejections and misses). Central to the approach is the assumption that even if the same memory strength signal is elicited by each probe, variation in testing procedures can render its expression more or less effective via measurement noise/error. For example, explicit recognition testing in a noisy environment might reduce measured accuracy relative to testing in a quiet environment through the addition of increased measurement error. In Figure 10.2 , this would be reflected by an increased spread of the two evidence distributions during noisy versus quiet conditions.</p>

<p>The next step in the approach is to specify the mapping of the strength variable ( f ) onto the speed of perceptual identification such that higher strength leads to quicker identification. In Berry et al. , it was assumed that there was a simple linear relationship between strength and reaction time (RT = b &ndash; sf + &#963; p ). Here, b and s are simply scaling parameters that would be different, for example, if reaction time was measured in seconds versus milliseconds. Critically, however, task-specific measurement error &#963; p is assumed different for the perceptual identification versus explicit recognition tasks, &#963; r , reflecting the standard measurement theory assumption that different measurement tasks are associated with independent measurement error. Thus, overall, the model assumes that performance during explicit recognition and perceptual identification rests on the same evidence variable f , which is greater for studied than novel items. This is what renders it a single-process account. As we briefly discuss below, the ability of the model to accommodate several single dissociations and unexpected reaction time patterns in the literature critically depends on its assumption that measurement error for explicit recognition tasks and perceptual identifications tasks is independent and different in magnitude.</p>

<p>Despite the simplicity of the single-process decision model, it can mimic several important findings. For example, it is compatible with the dissociable effects of levels of processing on explicit recognition (large effects) versus perceptual identification (small to nonexistent effects) if, during post hoc model fitting, one allows the measurement error during perceptual identification tests to become considerably larger than the measurement error during recognition testing. This is because with sufficiently large measurement error during the perceptual task, the levels of processing effects on the strength variable, f , are obscured during that task but clearly detectable during the explicit recognition task that has lower measurement error. Thus, under this framework, levels of processing effects are changing the strength signal during perceptual identification (because it is the same signal that drives recognition), but the perceptual tests are so noisy that these changes go undetected. Of course, this raises the question of why perceptual identification tests should be assumed to have such high measurement error. We return to this issue later.</p>

<p>A second phenomenon the single-process model accommodates is the so-called stochastic independence often observed across explicit recognition and priming scores. Documented early on by Tulving et al., stochastic independence is demonstrated when the rate of joint success on explicit recognition and priming tasks, at the level of subject&ndash;items, matches the product of the overall recognition rate and priming identification rate estimated separately. For example, in one condition of Tulving et al. , subjects were first tested on recognition for a list of intermixed studied and new items. In contrast, if priming and recognition were based on a single system or process, one might expect a positive dependency such that the joint probability of recognition and successful fragment completion would be higher than the product of the marginal, separate task success probabilities. However, as noted previously, the single-process model assumes separate measurement error for recognition and priming tasks, and critically, this error would limit the ability of correlations to emerge in these types of analyses. <p>Perhaps the most impressive finding accommodated by the single-system model is an effect that it initially appears to mispredict given the strength distributions in Figure 10.2 . Under the model, the strength variable, f , combined with perceptual measurement noise, &#963; p , determines the speed or success rate of responding during perceptual identification. That is, the speed of perceptual identification is a strictly increasing function of the total measured item strength. Thus, one expects hits to be more quickly perceptually identified than correct rejections, which reliably occurs. However, one would also expect false alarms to be more quickly identified than misses, which does not reliably occur and sometimes even reverses. This would seem to invalidate the model, but it does not because it overlooks the key role of measurement noise. Critically, under conditions of good recognition discrimination, both false alarms and misses reflect trials on which recognition measurement noise is likely to be exceptionally high and low, respectively. However, because recognition measurement noise and perceptual measurement noise are assumed independent, regressive effects will occur. That is, a new item that has an unusually high recognition measurement noise contribution spurring a false alarm is unlikely to have an equally unusually high noise contribution during an identification task, and an old item with an unusually low recognition measurement noise contribution spurring a miss is unlikely to have an equally unusually low perceptual noise contribution during an identification task. This regressive phenomenon hence pushes the total perceptual evidence values ( f + &#963; p ) closer together than suggested in , and through simulation, Berry et al. demonstrated the regressive effect can even produce identification speeds that are numerically slower for false alarms than misses despite arising from the single-strength process. Thus, as with the findings discussed above, the single-process model&rsquo;s success relies on its assumptions about independent task-related measurement noise.</p>

<p>Although the single-process account can mimic several important findings, it places a heavy explanatory burden on assumed noise phenomena that are neither well-specified nor directly manipulated. Of course, from a test theory perspective, there is no doubt that noise contributes to all psychological measurement techniques. However, to accommodate the data, the single-process approach often requires different assumptions about this measurement noise across different paradigms and subject populations, and these assumptions are often dictated solely by post hoc fitting or simulation of the observed patterns. For example, as noted above, dissociations between levels of processing effects on explicit recognition versus perceptual identification are accommodated by assuming the measurement noise is considerably higher during the perceptual identification task. In contrast, findings of relatively spared perceptual fluency effects in amnesics compared to near chance explicit recognition performance are accommodated by assuming that for amnesics, there is greater unsystematic variance (i.e., measurement noise) in the explicit recognition task than measurement error during the perceptual fluency task. Although these assumptions about measurement noise may prove to be valid, without any independent assessment of measurement noise, or falsifiable theories of the noise processes themselves, the single-process approach is potentially overflexible. Thus, although the findings briefly reviewed here constitute a useful proof of principle that a single evidence process plus independent noise sources can mimic observed data patterns, the model in its current state does not a priori demand those patterns. In addition, another major limitation of the single-process approach, acknowledged by its authors, is that it cannot accommodate situations in which a manipulation produces opposite effects on priming versus recognition (i.e., crossed dissociations). For example, Jacoby demonstrated that the generation of study items from antonyms produces greater recognition than simply reading subsequently tested words. However, the reverse pattern occurs for perceptual identification, in which reading facilitates identification compared to generation. This type of reversed effect cannot be accommodated under the basic single-process model because there is only a single genuine source of evidence driving performance.</p>

<p>Hybrid Approaches One potential way to reconcile the existence of crossed dissociations is to incorporate structured processing streams or stages into a model of repetition priming. Such structured processing stages are a common feature of language research, and a simple series of processing stages illustrating their utility can be seen in the left portion of Figure 10.4 . Here, it is assumed that semantic elaboration is preceded by word identification, which is preceded by visual feature analysis. Following visual feature analysis, the stimulus must be identified, which in the case of verbal recognition experiments requires orthographic, phonemic, and lexical processes. In conditions in which episodic memory is probed, the resulting identified word is used as a cue, in combination with a coarse representation of the target context, which potentially will trigger the retrieval of encoded episodic knowledge. Thus, in the case of the Jacoby crossed dissociation discussed above, one would assume that antonym generation less effectively primes word identification mechanisms than reading and hence produces less repetition-related facilitation for a subsequent perceptual identification test. In contrast, because antonym generation requires engagement of semantic elaboration, it facilitates tasks that benefit from the recovery of prior elaborations such as an explicit recognition test. These are the core ideas behind the transfer appropriate processing and encoding specificity frameworks. Thus, consideration of how the task (encoding and test) focuses attention on specific stages of the processing structure is capable of accommodating crossed dissociation findings. This focus on a structured processing stream does not preclude representational learning within a specific processing stage. Importantly, the illustration above assumes episodic learning only occurs at the end stages of this processing stream. To complete a hybrid model, it would be important to examine whether episodic learning plays any role in earlier stages of processing. For example, if the identification and/or semantic elaboration of a word could be newly bound to a decision as an episode for later retrieval, then repetition priming behavior could reflect this influence. This possibility motivated cognitive neuroscience researchers to examine the extent to which the behavior commonly observed during repetition priming paradigms in part depends on a form of instance learning in which a stimulus is bound to one of the output actions, such as a decision or a response.</p>

<p>Response/Decision Learning Cognitive neuroscience often applies noninvasive human neuroimaging methods in order to understand the neural basis of cognitive operations. Repetition priming is no exception, and typical repetition priming paradigms involving the use of perceptual identification or classification were examined early after the emergence of noninvasive neural imaging methods such as positron emission tomography and fMRI ( ; ). One of the earliest fMRI papers applied event-related fMRI methods to an object classification priming task ( ). In that paper, participants were asked during a study phase to make the judgment of &ldquo;whether this item moves or not&rdquo; to clip art pictures of common objects. The items were repeated six times during a study phase, which was followed by a test phase consisting of making the same judgment regarding items repeated from the study list and some novel items. As expected, participants were faster to make the movement judgment with item repetition. The study revealed fMRI signal reductions (i.e., repetition suppression) for repeated items relative to novel items in &ldquo;late visual regions&rdquo; of the ventral visual stream and, additionally, regions of supplementary motor cortex and dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex (PFC). Although no direct correlations were reported, the parallel findings of behavioral facilitation and neural activity reductions led the authors to conclude that the reductions reflected the &ldquo;neural correlates of priming.&rdquo; Most important, the locations of these activity reductions were taken as evidence for facilitation within specific cognitive components associated with object processing and decision-making. A number of studies provided similar evidence that task repetition was often accompanied by two parallel and reliable phenomenon&mdash;behavioral facilitation and reduced neural activity. An emerging framework for these findings was that these effects were largely thought to reflect small changes in representational systems that allowed for more focused access to goal-relevant information ( ).</p>

<p>A number of subsequent studies supported the findings of the Dobbins et al. paper and went further in revealing the extent to which stimulus&ndash;decision learning is associated with both PFC and ventral temporal activity reductions. Horner and Henson replicated the finding that PFC activity reductions were sensitive to changes in stimulus&ndash;response associations but also demonstrated that reductions in ventral temporal regions appeared to be preserved in the face of challenges to the stimulus&ndash;decision learning. Race et al. applied a paradigm that tried to further disentangle learning at three levels of representation: stimulus, decision, and response. The results revealed a complicated picture of neural activity reductions in lateral frontal cortex indicating associations with stimulus-specific representations, retrieval of learned stimulus&ndash;decision mappings, and retrieval of learned stimulus&ndash;response mappings. They concluded that the findings, along with those of the work by Horner and Henson , supported a &ldquo;multi-process model of neural priming.&rdquo; Finally, Horner and Henson examined the level of abstraction at which stimulus&ndash;response bindings can incur. Although they found evidence for learning across multiple levels of abstraction, there was stronger binding for within-format repetitions relative to between-format repetitions. In the end, a number of research studies supported the finding that when a repetition priming paradigm encouraged the learning of new stimulus&ndash;decision or stimulus&ndash;response associations, those bindings would facilitate subsequent performance.</p>

<p>Returning to an earlier focus of the priming literature, it remained unclear as to whether stimulus&ndash;decision or stimulus&ndash;response learning required an intact episodic memory system&mdash;in other words, to what extent this form of learning was related to new episodic learning. On their face, these instances seem to be the very definition of an episode; however, it is also possible that they exist as traces without conscious awareness of the prior events and so would not be episodic in nature. Prior formulations do not seem to have directly addressed this issue. Therefore, one question that arises from the work on response learning is whether the formation of new stimulus&ndash;action bindings requires the neural system associated with new episodic learning&mdash;namely medial temporal lobe (MTL) structures including the hippocampus. Examining this question, Schnyer et al. revealed that the MTL was indeed required for new stimulus&ndash;decision learning. Ten amnesic participants with radiologically verified MTL lesions engaged in a stimulus&ndash;decision learning paradigm similar to that used in the study by Dobbins et al. . The patients in that study did not show stimulus&ndash;decision learning, as evidenced by two key patterns in the data. First, they failed to show the monotonic increases in facilitation associated with additional exposures during the initial task repetitions as evident in controls. Second, later inversion of the decision question had no effect on their level of priming, whereas it markedly reduced that of the controls. The failure of the amnesics to show either interference during question inversion or gains beyond a single repetition during the initial repetitions indicated they lacked a form of learning present in the controls, and supported by MTL regions, namely stimulus&ndash;decision learning. However, these amnesic patients did demonstrate a mild facilitation in performance relative to entirely novel stimuli. This facilitation may reflect small changes in local representations that do not require an intact MTL, such as the maintenance relearning formulated by Marsolek .</p>

<p>Since the initial report, two additional studies have emerged examining the question of MTL dependence of stimulus&ndash;decision learning. In a subsequent study, this dependence was challenged when it was revealed that reversing response contingencies abolished priming equally for both six patients with selective hippocampal lesions and matched controls, supporting the contention that stimulus&ndash;decision learning is preserved in patients with MTL lesions. There were multiple methodological differences between the two studies that could have contributed to their different results, but one possible source of the discrepancy between these two findings was potentially clarified with an additional study of 12 MTL damaged patients using a paradigm designed to dissociate stimulus&ndash;task, stimulus&ndash;decision, and stimulus&ndash;response levels of learning.</p>

<p>Race et al. found that stimulus&ndash;task and stimulus&ndash;decision bindings were impaired with MTL damage but that stimulus&ndash;response bindings were intact. The authors concluded that stimulus&ndash;response-level learning may be supported by the striatum or premotor cortex through a process of procedural or motor learning. An interesting additional finding of this study was evidence of an interference effect in controls, but not patients, when both the task and the response between study and test were altered. The interference effect, manifested as response slowing for repetitions relative to novel items, indicating evidence of prior learning that required the need to exert additional cognitive control in order to resolve the conflict between the previously learned associations and current goals. The lack of an interference effect in amnesic patients is further evidence that they were not burdened by having learned the stimulus&ndash;task-level bindings. This finding is an example of where the lack of a more formalized model led to the failure to anticipate contexts in which impairment instead of facilitation of behavior would be encountered.</p>

<p>The work on stimulus&ndash;decision/stimulus&ndash;response learning revealed solid evidence for the influence of multiple newly formed associations that can facilitate or impair behavior in a typical repetition priming task. In addition, these associations appear to be overlaid on top of faciliatory changes that are assumed to reflect alterations in specific processing stages (visual features, word identification, and semantics). Therefore, it becomes important to consider how task demands alter the relative contributions of these priming mechanisms. Such a model would need to reflect opportunistic learning throughout the processing stream. Although there is strong evidence for local representational changes that might reflect the kind of maintenance relearning postulated by Marsolek that is required of distributed neurocognitive systems, there is also now considerable evidence of the ability to learn new bindings between representations at specific processing stages and subsequent actions (decision and response). The former has been traditionally associated with repetition priming and appears largely unaffected in patients with damage to the MTL. The latter appears to be dependent on episodic and/or motor learning systems. One final aspect to consider is whether there are fundamental aspects of the neural architecture that promote bindings at specific levels of processing. To address this question, Saggar et al. examined whether a mechanism of decision learning could be induced by repeating decisions on novel visual objects that had never been seen prior to the experiment. The work clearly revealed that stimulus&ndash;decision learning cannot be induced for items for which there were no pre-existing representations. For the time being, we would need to assume that new procedural or episodic bindings may not be possible at the lowest processing stages, such as the visual feature level ( Figure 10.4 ).</p>

<p>Figure 10.4 The adaptive learning model of priming: a proposed descriptive model of long-term repetition priming. The model identifies a series of processing stages that lead from the identification of visual features through semantic knowledge. Although three levels are shown, there could be multiple additional stages that reflect underlying representational systems recruited for different tasks and different modalities. Within any given stage, local changes in network properties that reflect strengthening of specific representations can yield facilitated processing associated with repetition. In addition to these relatively small changes, task demands often encourage other, more generalized learning systems to create new linkages between stimulus representations and rendered decisions or stimulus and responses. These new linkages have been considered under the framework of instance learning or, more recently, response learning. By creating these linkages, the individual can preserve deliberative executive processing resources and adopt a more automated approach to the task, thus resulting in significant behavioral facilitation, neural activity savings, and the ability to engage in other deliberative tasks if necessary.</p>

<p>Finally, it is important to consider that the general purpose (or a key benefit) of learning new task-related associations is to reduce executive or control processing demands on the organism. This manifests as a form of automaticity and would imply that there would be costs associated with the need to re-engage control when the processing demands change. There was evidence for this in Dobbins et al. . In that paper, during the block of trials following cue inversion where there was reinstatement of the original cue, participants were readily capable of reapplying the learned stimulus&ndash;decision associations to their benefit. This shows the durability of this type of learning. A recent paper addressed this more directly by showing that at retrieval, participants are somehow able to moderate the influence of stimulus&ndash;response associations separately for stimulus&ndash;decision mappings and stimulus&ndash;motor mappings. In this sense, there appears to be some awareness in participants that they are behaving relatively automatically and they can therefore act to override this when feedback signals it is too costly. This finding adds to the complexity of repetition priming and underscores the need to build a more precise &ldquo;hybrid&rdquo; model.</p>

<p>The Adaptive Learning Model of Repetition Priming In the past, investigators tended to pit one theory of repetition priming against another in a winner-take-all fashion. The same appears to be the case with respect to repetition suppression effects. It seems evident that there is consistent and reliable support for a more multisystem, multimechanism explanation for long-term repetition priming. A preliminary approach is suggested in the adaptive learning model of repetition priming. Although this model emerges as an early stage descriptive model, it nonetheless is able to synthesize the broad range of findings in the literature. The schematic presented here is based on a simple visual object classification task, such as those used in a broad range of priming experiments. The modules could be replaced with other stimuli, other modalities, as well as other decision/task demands. The fundamental assumptions of the model are that current decisions or actions will be associated with the earliest stimulus representations possible given the task constraints, associative learning processes free executive resources for other deliberative tasks, and local representational changes also occur with stimulus repetition. From this perspective, both unified and multisystem explanations are partially correct.</p>

<p>Conclusion In the end, the question is not whether a mechanism or system of learning is exclusively engaged during a repetition priming paradigm but, rather, what altered aspect of a structured &ldquo;system&rdquo; or set of &ldquo;processes&rdquo; does priming reflect. In that regard, a fundamental principle of the brain and the behavior it affords is to gain efficiencies as one navigates through the world, but in a way that is as contextually flexible as feasible and frees up effortful control processes. One manner in which this flexibility is achieved is through the allocation of attention in a goal-linked manner. It is in this manner that the system is highly adaptive because it can regulate the relative degree to which response, decision, or motor association learning should contribute to subsequent task performance. However, subtle changes to underlying representations as a form of maintenance relearning and strengthening of those representations will also occur in tandem, as a function of the structure of information processing in relation to task demands. Critically, both aspects of learning (local/representational vs. associative) are important for understanding facilitation versus impairment in new environments (i.e., generalization).</p>
